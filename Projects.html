Rivers and the PETM

About 56 million years ago, a massive release of carbon warmed Earth by more than 5 °C. This event, the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM), is one of the best geologic analogs for modern climate change, so how rivers responded to it can tell us how rivers might respond to future warming.

I study PETM river deposits in the Bighorn Basin of northwestern Wyoming with Brady Foreman (Western Washington University) and Akshay Mehra (University of Washington). We combine field sedimentology with drone photogrammetry, building three-dimensional point clouds of outcrops to measure sedimentary structures remotely, and we use paleocurrents to reconstruct the planform of ancient rivers.

So far, the rivers do not appear to have gotten bigger during the PETM: we see no clear change in flow depth, channel planform, or the flow conditions recorded by bedforms. Instead, they reworked their floodplains faster and left behind a much wider channel belt. This matters because about two billion people live on floodplains, and a river that shifts across its floodplain more quickly can erode the farms, homes, roads, and bridges along its banks, even if the river itself gets no bigger. That pace also sets how long sediment and carbon are stored on floodplains, with major implications for the carbon cycle.

We currently have two papers in review, so check back soon to take a look at those!

Ooids

Ooids are one of the most common constituents of the carbonate rock record, and there is a long history of trying to use ooid size, shape, abundance, and mineralogy to reconstruct the conditions of the ancient ocean. These attempts have been hindered by the inability to make accurate measurements of ooid size and shape.

Through a series of numerical experiments, I realized that measuring a three-dimensional grain in two dimensions (e.g., in thin sections and polished slabs) introduces errors that overwhelm the environmental signals encoded in the morphology of ooids. I then developed a method using GIRI to measure ooids and ooid growth bands in three dimensions.

In a follow-up project, I measured the growth histories of ooids by tracing their laminations. I found that many of the popular models of ooid growth have been overestimating the role of abrasion in the formation of ooids. With these new insights into ooid formation, it became possible to better explain the geographic distribution of ooids and their abundance through the Phanerozoic.

Papers: Howes et al. 2021, JGR: Earth Surface · Mehra et al. 2022, Microscopy and Microanalysis · Howes et al. 2024, EPSL

K–Pg

The extinction at the K–Pg boundary is the most recent mass extinction, and its aftermath gave rise to the flora and fauna that dominate modern terrestrial ecosystems. However, there are very few terrestrial records of the extinction and recovery outside of North America. I have been working in the Potosí Basin in Bolivia alongside collaborators from the Universidad Mayor de San Andrés (UMSA) to constrain the position of the K–Pg boundary in Bolivia and improve our understanding of the extinction and recovery.

We have used drones to map outcrops, make photo-orthomosaics, and test for cyclicity in the sediments. This cyclicity has been calibrated with U–Pb zircon ages measured in the Schoene Lab at Princeton and provides the backbone for the chronostratigraphy of the Potosí Basin.

The sedimentological and geochronological work provides important context for the exciting paleontological work being led by teams from UMSA, the University of Washington, the University of Michigan, and the Denver Museum of Nature & Science.

Papers: Velez-Rosado et al. 2025, Journal of Systematic Palaeontology

Source-to-Sink in the Red Sea

In my research on the Red Sea, I analyze geological and climatic patterns recorded in the sedimentary record. I start by reconstructing historical rainfall from remote sensing and archival records. I focus on identifying intense rainfall events that can lead to flash floods, given their significant risks to communities and infrastructure and their likelihood of contributing to the deep-sea sediment record via hyperpycnal flows. With this reconstruction, we can evaluate the fidelity and completeness of the deep-sea sedimentary record as a recorder of extreme rainfall on land.

I have also been working towards understanding how lowstand deposits from the last glacial interval are being destroyed through erosion and mass wasting, despite sitting ~120 m below sea level.

Papers: Duchâtellier et al. 2024, Science of the Total Environment · Chakraborty et al. 2025, PNAS · Purkis et al. 2025, Science Advances · Chakraborty et al. 2026, AGU Advances
